# Extreme Heat Days: Guidance & Methods

## What is an extreme heat day?

An extreme heat day is defined here as a day in which the maximum daily
temperature exceeds the selected temperature threshold, whether it's an absolute
threshold or a percentile-based threshold (e.g. 99th). Users can select other
thresholds to explore extreme heat days relevant to their local climate. The
number of extreme heat days per year is calculated for historical and future
global warming levels.

## What is a warm night?

A warm night is defined here as a day in which the minimum daily temperature
exceeds the selected temperature threshold, whether it's an absolute threshold or
a percentile-based threshold (e.g. 99th). Users can select other thresholds to
explore warm nights relevant to their local climate. The number of warm nights
per year is calculated for historical and future global warming levels.

## What are global warming levels?

A global warming level is defined as the increase in the global mean air
temperature relative to pre-industrial conditions (1850-1900)[@ipcc-allen-special-report-2022].
Global warming levels are frequently used in international
policy discussions (for example, goals to constrain global warming to 1.5 or
2 °C). Global warming levels can also be useful in planning processes for
comparing potential regional responses to climate change across different
warming levels, enabling comparison of models across emissions scenarios. The
standard global warming levels are 1.5 °C, 2 °C, 3 °C, and 4 °C.

Which global warming levels will be reached and how quickly depends on societal
actions around greenhouse gases in the coming decades. The IPCC has developed a
series of possible scenarios, the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), that
illustrate a range of possible timelines for future emissions and global
temperatures. Each SSP scenario provides an estimated timeline for when each GWL
will be reached.

## How should temperature thresholds be used?

Using absolute thresholds will answer the question of how many days per year will
exceed 95 °F? In contrast, using a percentile-based threshold will answer the
question of: how many days per year will exceed the historically-defined hottest
temperatures for the selected region?

Percentile-based thresholds are useful for defining extreme heat days across a
broad range of geographic locations with unique climates. For example, a high
elevation region may not experience 95 °F, but the historical 99th percentile
temperature still represents an impactful extreme heat day. These 99th percentile
extreme heat events represent 3-4 of the hottest days per year _on average_.

## Limitations

This definition of extreme heat days only uses daily temperature data. Several
other meteorological variables and environmental factors can influence the
impacts of heat on human health and infrastructure, including humidity, wind
speed, shading, the built environment, and the timing or duration of heat events.

## Methods

The sections above describe what extreme heat days and warm nights mean and how
to use them. The steps below describe exactly how those numbers were calculated,
from raw climate model output to the values shown in the tool.

### Input data

<Specs>
<Spec label="Variables">

Maximum daily air temperature (extreme heat days), minimum daily air
temperature (warm nights)

</Spec>
<Spec label="Spatial resolution">

3 kilometer gridscale resolution

</Spec>
<Spec label="Data products">

ERA5 historical reanalysis data and ensemble of 4 bias-adjusted climate model
simulations, dynamically downscaled using the WRF model for the state of
California: `EC-Earth3-r1i1p1f1`, `MPI-ESM1-2-HR-r3i1p1f1`, `TaiESM1-r1i1p1f1`,
`MIROC6-r1i1p1f1`.

</Spec>
</Specs>

<Step n={1} totalSteps={7} title="Data retrieval and preprocessing">

Using Cal-Adapt's open source Python library `climakitae`, input data was
retrieved. Climate model simulations were retrieved for historical and future
global warming levels (GWL): 0.8, 1.5, 2.5, and 3.0 °C. This retrieval process
pulls the 30 year time window around the year (+/- 15 years) in which the global
warming level is reached. ERA5 historical reanalysis data was retrieved for
1987-2017, corresponding to GWL 0.8.

</Step>

<Step n={2} totalSteps={7} title="Percentile-based threshold">

For percentile-based thresholds, a per-gridcell historical temperature threshold
was calculated using the 0.8 GWL for each simulation. This value represents an
extreme heat threshold unique to the historical conditions at each location.

</Step>

<Step n={3} totalSteps={7} title="Number of exceedance days in each year">

For each threshold (e.g. 95 °F, 100 °F, 99th percentile), the **number of days
in each year** that the maximum (extreme heat days) or minimum (warm nights)
daily temperature exceeds the threshold was counted. The exceedance days were
calculated for each simulation and GWL.

<Callout title="Plain language example">

If a specific location has a historical 99th percentile maximum temperature of
97 °F, this represents an "extreme heat" day in the historical period. Step 3 asks:
under \+1.5 °C of global warming, how many days in each year in that gridcell
will exceed 97 °F? When asking this question using a fixed temperature
threshold, it changes to: how many days per year will this gridcell exceed
95 °F? 100 °F?

</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={4} totalSteps={7} title="Median and variability over GWL time window">

The following metrics were calculated for each simulation across the 30-year
period corresponding to each GWL: median, 10th percentile, 90th percentile. This
collapses the time dimension, yielding a single value per metric per simulation.

<Callout title="Why compute the 10th and 90th percentile?">

The 10th and 90th percentile values represent the number of extreme heat days on
a particularly cold year and particularly hot year, respectively. Together,
these provide an estimate of the range of year-to-year variability of extreme
heat under future conditions. These 10th and 90th percentiles specifically
represent conditions that would be expected to occur once every 10 years on
average.

</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={5} totalSteps={7} title="Change signal">

For each GWL above the reference period (1.5 °C and higher) and for each metric
(median, 10th percentile, 90th percentile), the historical baseline value was
subtracted from the future GWL value. This difference represents the projected
change in extreme heat days relative to the historical period.

<Callout title="Plain language example">

If a grid cell experiences 6 days of extreme heat (say, above 105 °F) in the
historical period, how many **more** days of extreme heat will that same grid
cell experience under \+1.5 °C (and 2.0 and so on) of global warming?

</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={6} totalSteps={7} title="Spatial aggregation">

Next, we aggregated the gridded data by spatial unit (e.g. county, city, etc) by
taking a **median** of all the grid cells within the boundary. A median is used
to capture the typical number of extreme heat days within the area of interest
without introducing bias from any anomalous grid cells.

<Callout title="Grid cells at the edge">

All grid cells within or touching the spatial boundary were included (even if not
fully contained within the boundary) to avoid discarding data representative of
conditions at the edge. Additionally, small spatial units such as census tracts
may not fully contain any single grid cell, so an all-touched approach is
generally used across Cal Adapt for consistency across spatial scales.

</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={7} totalSteps={7} title="Simulation aggregation">

Finally, the data was aggregated across simulations using a median. The final
data product is a table for each county containing the following variables:

#### Absolute variables

- Number of annual exceedance days (for each GWL and threshold)
- 10th percentile number of annual exceedance days (for each GWL and threshold)
- 90th percentile number of annual exceedance days (for each GWL and threshold)

#### Relative change variables

- Change from reference period: number of annual exceedance days (for each GWL and threshold)
- Change from reference period: 10th percentile number of annual exceedance days (for each GWL and threshold)
- Change from reference period: 90th percentile number of annual exceedance days (for each GWL and threshold)

</Step>
