# HDD/CDD: Guidance & Methods

## What are heating and cooling degree days?

Heating degree days (HDD) and cooling degree days (CDD) measure how much and
for how long outdoor air temperature is below or above a reference
temperature, called the base temperature. They are the standard way the
energy sector estimates how much heating or cooling a building will need on a
given day: colder days accumulate more HDD, and hotter days accumulate more
CDD. Summed over a year, HDD and CDD track how climate change is expected to
shift electricity and gas demand for heating and cooling across California.

## Why is the threshold fixed at 65°F?

65°F is the conventional base temperature used across the energy industry for
degree-day calculations — it approximates the outdoor temperature at which a
typical building needs neither heating nor cooling. This tool uses a single
fixed 65°F threshold for both HDD and CDD. A configurable threshold may be
added in a future iteration.

## Why do these trends matter?

As the climate warms, heating demand is generally expected to fall and
cooling demand to rise, but the timing and magnitude differ by location.
Tracking HDD and CDD over time helps identify which parts of the state will
see the fastest growth in electricity demand from cooling, and where heating
demand is easing — both of which shape utility planning, rate design, and
grid reliability.

## What is the SSP3-7.0 scenario?

The chart shows a continuous timeseries from 1981 through 2099: historical
conditions through 2014, followed by a projection under the SSP3-7.0
scenario from 2015 onward. SSPs (Shared Socioeconomic Pathways) are a set of
standardized future scenarios used across climate science to represent
different trajectories of greenhouse gas emissions[@ipcc-allen-special-report-2022].
SSP3-7.0 represents a moderate-to-high emissions pathway. Additional
scenarios (e.g. SSP2-4.5, SSP5-8.5) may be added in future iterations.

## Limitations

HDD and CDD are calculated from air temperature alone. They do not account
for humidity, building insulation and design, occupancy patterns, or
electrification of heating and cooling — all of which also shape real-world
energy demand. A single statewide 65°F threshold may not reflect local
building stock or climate norms in every region.

## Methods

The sections above describe what HDD and CDD mean and how to use them. The
steps below describe exactly how those numbers were calculated, from raw
climate model output to the values shown in the tool.

### Input data

<Specs>
<Spec label="Variable">

Daily average air temperature (t2)

</Spec>
<Spec label="Spatial resolution">

3 kilometer gridscale resolution

</Spec>
<Spec label="Data products">

Ensemble of 4 bias-adjusted climate model simulations, dynamically
downscaled using the WRF model for the state of California:
`EC-Earth3-r1i1p1f1`, `MPI-ESM1-2-HR-r3i1p1f1`, `TaiESM1-r1i1p1f1`,
`MIROC6-r1i1p1f1`, under the SSP3-7.0 scenario, 1981-2100.

</Spec>
</Specs>

<Step n={1} totalSteps={5} title="Data retrieval">

Using Cal-Adapt's open source Python library `climakitae`, daily average air
temperature was retrieved for each of the 4 bias-adjusted WRF models for the
full 1981-2100 SSP3-7.0 period.

</Step>

<Step n={2} totalSteps={5} title="Compute HDD and CDD">

For each day and each simulation, HDD65 and CDD65 were computed from daily
average temperature using a fixed 65°F threshold, via `climakitae`'s built-in
`compute_hdd_cdd` function.

</Step>

<Step n={3} totalSteps={5} title="Annual sums">

Daily HDD and CDD were trimmed to complete calendar years only, then summed
within each year to produce one HDD and one CDD total per year, per
simulation.

<Callout title="Plain language example">

If a location accumulates 5 heating degree days on a cold day and 0 on a mild
day, HDD for the year is the sum of the daily values across all 365 (or 366)
days in that year.

</Callout>

</Step>

<Step n={4} totalSteps={5} title="Spatial aggregation">

The gridded annual HDD/CDD data was aggregated by spatial unit (e.g. county,
watershed, forecast zone, or electric balancing area) by taking the
**median** of all grid cells within the boundary, for each simulation and
year.

</Step>

<Step n={5} totalSteps={5} title="Simulation aggregation">

Finally, for each region and year, the data was aggregated across the 4
simulations by taking the **mean**, **minimum**, and **maximum** across
models. This produces the multi-model mean line and min/max range shown in
the chart. A mean/min/max convention (rather than percentiles) is used here
because there are only 4 simulations available.

</Step>
